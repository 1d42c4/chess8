The King’s Indian contract

The King’s Indian is easier to understand if you stop thinking of it as “Black gives White the center and attacks.” The actual bargain is:

White accepts

More space, a large pawn center, and often a queenside initiative. In return, the center can become a target and White may have less room around the king once Black’s pawn breaks arrive.

Black accepts

Less space and some temporary cramp. In return, Black gets a clear target—the pawn center—and dynamic breaks with ...e5, ...c5, and often ...f5.

First diagnostic: before thinking about a tactic, ask: Is the center open, fluid, or locked? That answer tells you whether wing attacks are justified.

Read the pawn chains, not the opening name

In the classic locked structure, White’s pawn chain often points toward the queenside and Black’s chain toward the kingside. That gives each side a natural direction, but it is a starting hypothesis, not a law.

Black: a practical decision tree

  1. Finish king safety. Usually ...Nf6, ...g6, ...Bg7, ...d6, ...O-O.
  2. Challenge the center. Choose between ...e5 and ...c5 based on White’s setup.
  3. If White closes with d5: reroute the f6-knight if needed, prepare ...f5, and count attackers before pushing further.
  4. If the center opens: stop thinking “pawn storm” and activate the g7 bishop, rooks, and tactical pressure against e4/d4.
  5. If White attacks your queenside: ask whether defending it is necessary. In many KID positions, counterplay against the king is worth more than saving every pawn.

White: space must become something

White’s extra space is useful only if it creates targets, files, or better squares. The practical goal is usually to make Black spend tempi on the queenside while keeping the kingside sufficiently closed.

Classical / Mar del Plata

Prepare c5, often with b4. Open the c-file, attack d6/c7, and look for penetration squares.

Bayonet

b4 comes quickly. White grabs space before Black’s pieces are ideally placed and can force ...a5 or ...c5 concessions.

Sämisch

f3 supports e4 and can prepare Be3/Qd2. White may attack on the kingside or castle long, but must respect ...c5 and central blows.

Fianchetto

g3/Bg2 makes the king more robust and changes Black’s normal attack. White plays more positionally against d5/e5 breaks.

The race: count tempi, not material

The Mar del Plata is a useful training ground because both sides often know where they are going. That makes time unusually important.

  • White’s queenside play often wins pawns first. That does not mean White is winning if Black is one move from opening the king.
  • Black’s attack should gain tempi by hitting pieces or forcing pawn moves. Slow decorative moves can lose the race.
  • When one side’s attack is against a king and the other side’s attack is against pawns, the king-side attack often has greater urgency.
Training habit: in a model game, stop every three moves and write one sentence: “White needs ___ before Black gets ___.” That teaches timing better than memorizing the move numbers.

White Nc3/Nf3—Often reroute to d3/e1; defend e4 and support c5
Recall: Why can ...Rf7 be stronger than putting a rook on an already-open file?
Because it prepares a future file/rank before it exists and coordinates with ...Bf8, ...Rg7/...Rh7. The attack is built before it is visible.
Recall: What should White do while Black reroutes pieces?
Use the tempi: improve queenside pieces, prepare c5/b5, and create concrete entry squares rather than making waiting moves.

Anti-KID systems: understand the personality

You do not need a telephone-book repertoire to start. You need to recognize what White’s setup is trying to deny.

White systemWhat White wantsWhat Black must remember
Classical Be2 + Nf3Flexible development; often close center and race...e5 and ...f5 are core; learn the locked structure deeply
Bayonet 9.b4Accelerate queenside expansion before Black is readyDo not lose tempi; ...Nh5, ...a5, ...c5 ideas are structural choices
Sämisch f3Very strong e4, flexible king, potential kingside attack...c5 is often an important counter; hit the center, not just the king
Fianchetto g3Safer king; reduce force of normal kingside stormBe more positional; central and queenside counterplay matter more
Exchange dxe5Simplify and reduce attacking chancesAccept a more technical game; activate pieces, do not force a fake attack
The goal for a first repertoire is one dependable response + one structural idea against each family, not exhaustive theory.

Model games: how to study them

Do not watch model games like movies. Use this three-pass method:

  1. Pass 1 — direction: click through quickly and say where each side is playing.
  2. Pass 2 — prediction: stop before every pawn break and predict it.
  3. Pass 3 — reconstruction: return to move 9–12 and try to reconstruct the next five moves from the position.

Success criterion: after studying, you should be able to explain why ...f5, c5, b4, ...Rf7, or a knight retreat belongs—not merely remember that it happened.

The mistakes this course is trying to prevent

Black autopilot

Playing ...f5-f4-g5-g4 regardless of center, piece count, or White’s setup.

White “space = advantage”

Owning more squares but never opening a file or creating a target before Black’s attack arrives.

Material tunnel vision

Grabbing queenside pawns while ignoring that the black queen, rook, bishop, and knight are entering the king zone.

Theory without position

Remembering 14.c5 but not knowing whether c5 is still good after one earlier move changed the structure.

10-minute KID review loop

  1. 2 min: reconstruct the basic setup from memory.
  2. 2 min: say the three Black questions: center? break? worst piece?
  3. 2 min: say the three White questions: where is my space? what file can I open? how many tempi until Black opens my king?
  4. 2 min: replay one model game from move 9 to move 20, predicting pawn breaks.
  5. 2 min: answer one quiz section without looking.

If you can explain the plans but still forget move orders, that is a much easier problem to fix than knowing move orders without understanding the plans.
